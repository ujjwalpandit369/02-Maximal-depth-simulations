Beginner's Guide

Have you ever seen a flat map of the world, like the Mercator projection? You probably noticed that Greenland looks enormous, bigger than Africa, even though it's much smaller. The map is distorted. To fit the curved, spherical Earth onto a flat piece of paper, we have to stretch and squeeze different parts of it. A Penrose diagram does the same thing, but for something even trickier to map: an entire, infinite universe (or "spacetime").

Step 1: The Problem of Infinity
Spacetime, as described by Einstein's theories, is big. Really big. It can be infinite in extent and last for an infinite amount of time. How can you possibly draw a picture of that? You can't just draw a normal graph, because you'd need an infinitely large piece of paper. This is a problem if we want to understand the "big picture" of a spacetime—where did it begin? Where does it end? What parts can send signals to what other parts? To see the whole story, we need a map of infinity.

Step 2: The Cartographer's Recipe
This simulation shows you the three-step "recipe" for making one of these maps. You are the cartographer!

  1. Convert to Null Coordinates: Press the first button. Instead of a grid of time and space (t, x), we switch to a grid based on the paths of light rays (u, v). This is like turning the map 45 degrees. It's a convenient first step for the math that comes next. Notice how the grid in Panel B is now made of diamonds.
  2. Apply Range Compression: Press the second button. This is the magic trick for taming infinity. We apply a mathematical function (specifically `arctan`) to our coordinates. This function takes any number, no matter how large (positive or negative infinity), and "squishes" its output into a small, finite range. This forces our entire infinite grid into the finite diamond shape you see in Panel B. The points that used to be "at infinity" are now the very edges of this diamond!
  3. Perform Conformal Rescaling: Press the third button. There's one problem with the compressed map in Panel B: it's very distorted. If you draw a light ray (a yellow line), you'll see its path is curved in Panel B. The final step is a clever rescaling that "un-bends" the light rays, making them straight 45-degree lines again. This is the most important feature of a Penrose diagram. It preserves causal structure, meaning you can easily see where light can and can't travel.

Use the Geodesic Tracer to draw lines on the normal spacetime in Panel A. A yellow line is a light ray. Notice how it's a straight 45-degree line in Panel A and also a straight 45-degree line in the final Penrose Diagram in Panel C. This is the power of these maps: they show the entire infinite spacetime while making the paths of light rays simple and clear.

The Physics and Mathematics of Conformal Mapping

Part A: Why Map Spacetime? The Problem of Infinity

In General Relativity, a spacetime is not just a stage; it's a dynamic object whose geometry is determined by matter and energy. To truly understand a given solution to Einstein's equations, we need to understand its global and causal structure. This means answering questions like: Does it contain singularities (places where the theory breaks down)? Does it have event horizons? Can an observer starting at event A ever reach event B? A standard coordinate chart, like the (t, x) coordinates for Minkowski space, is often "local" and fails to capture the full picture, especially when infinities are involved. Penrose diagrams are a tool to visualize this global structure. By bringing points at infinity to a finite distance on the diagram, we can see the entire spacetime, from its infinite past (past timelike infinity, i⁻) to its infinite future (future timelike infinity, i⁺) and its infinitely distant regions (spacelike infinity, i⁰), all at a glance.

Part B: A Step-by-Step Mathematical Guide

The construction of a Penrose diagram for 2D Minkowski space (metric: ds² = -dt² + dx²) is a sequence of coordinate transformations.

  1. Null Coordinates (u, v): We define two new coordinates based on light rays moving left and right.
    u = t - x
    v = t + x
    In these coordinates, the metric becomes ds² = -du dv. Lines of constant `u` and `v` are null geodesics (paths of light). The entire spacetime is covered by `u` and `v` ranging from -∞ to +∞.
  2. Range Compression (U, V): To map the infinite range of u and v to a finite interval, we use the arctangent function.
    U = arctan(u)
    V = arctan(v)
    Now, U and V are confined to the range (-π/2, π/2). The entire infinite spacetime is now contained within a finite diamond shape on the (U, V) plane.
  3. Penrose Coordinates (T, X): Finally, we rotate the compressed diamond back by 45 degrees for convention and clarity.
    T = U + V
    X = V - U
    The metric in these final coordinates can be shown to be ds² = Ω²(-dT² + dX²), where Ω is the conformal factor.

Part C: The Magic of Conformal Invariance

The final transformation is a conformal transformation. This means it rescales all local distances by a position-dependent factor, Ω², but it crucially preserves angles. In spacetime, the most important "angles" are defined by the light cone. The paths of light rays leaving a point form a "light cone" with an opening angle of 90 degrees (45 degrees in each direction on our diagrams). Because a Penrose diagram is conformally related to the original spacetime, the local light cones are always preserved as perfect 90-degree V-shapes everywhere on the diagram. This means that even though vast regions of spacetime have been compressed, you can still tell at a glance what is causally connected. A path is timelike if its tangent is within the light cone (< 45 degrees from the vertical), null if it's on the cone (at 45 degrees), and spacelike if it's outside the cone (> 45 degrees).

Advanced Notes & Extensions

1. Generalizing to 4D Spacetimes

This simulation uses 2D spacetimes (1 time + 1 space dimension) for simplicity. To create a Penrose diagram for a realistic 4D spacetime (like our universe, or a 4D black hole), we typically assume spherical symmetry. This allows us to suppress the two angular dimensions (θ, φ) and draw a 2D diagram where every point on the diagram implicitly represents a 2-sphere (the surface of a sphere). For example, the central vertical line (r=0) in a black hole diagram represents the origin, while the singularity is a line in the diagram representing a point in space evolving over time. The event horizon, which is a 3D surface in spacetime (a 2-sphere evolving in time), is represented as a 2D surface on the diagram.

2. The Conformal Factor Ω

The conformal factor Ω relates the physical metric `g` to the unphysical metric `g_unphys` of the Penrose diagram: `g = Ω² * g_unphys`. It essentially tells you how much the local scale has been stretched or shrunk at each point to fit the spacetime onto the page. The boundaries of the Penrose diagram are where Ω becomes zero. For example, in the Minkowski diagram, future timelike infinity (i⁺) is the point where all future-directed timelike lines converge. This is a point where Ω=0. A singularity, like the one inside a Schwarzschild black hole, is a boundary where physical curvature blows up, and this also corresponds to a boundary on the Penrose diagram.

3. Alternative Compression Functions

While `arctan(x)` is the standard choice for compressing an infinite range, other functions can be used, leading to diagrams with different visual appearances. For example, a function like `tanh(x)` (hyperbolic tangent) also maps (-∞, ∞) to a finite range (-1, 1). Using `tanh` instead of `arctan` would result in a diagram with differently curved grid lines, but the same overall topology and boundary structure. The choice of function is a matter of mathematical convenience and aesthetic preference for the final appearance of the diagram.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

What do the different line colors mean when I draw a path?
The color indicates the causal nature of the path you drew in Panel A. Red lines are "timelike" (slower than light, a path a massive object could take). Yellow lines are "null" (the path of a light ray). Blue lines are "spacelike" (faster than light, not a physically traversable path).
Why is the Rindler diagram just a triangle?
The Rindler coordinate system only covers a "wedge" of the full Minkowski spacetime. Specifically, it describes the spacetime as seen by a uniformly accelerating observer who can never receive signals from or send signals to certain parts of the universe. The boundaries of the triangle are the "Rindler horizons."
